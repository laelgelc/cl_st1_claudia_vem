**Discursive Constructions of COIL Virtual Exchange in Institutional
Communication: A Corpus-Based Study**

### **Abstract**

This article analyzes the discursive recontextualization of
Collaborative Online International Learning (COIL) and International
Collaborative Projects (PCIs) in the VEm (Virtual Exchange Medium)
newsletter of Colleges of Technology within a Brazilian higher education
institution. Drawing on a corpus-based approach, keywords were extracted
from 33 issues of the newsletter, refined through dispersion criteria,
and examined using concordance analysis. The findings reveal three main
discursive formations: (1) Institutional Management of COIL Projects;
(2) IaH; and (3) Technological Education and Vocational Training. The
results indicate that COIL/PCI practices are embedded in a hybrid
discursive field in which intercultural and pedagogical ideals of
internationalization coexist with managerial, vocational, and
organizational rationalities. Within this configuration,
Internationalization at Home (IaH), Virtual Exchange, and COIL are
recontextualized as PCI practices, which are shaped by institutional
logics of management, applicability, and temporal organization within
the aforementioned colleges. Results show that virtual
internationalization is not a neutral or stable pedagogical model, but
rather a discursively mediated practice shaped by local institutional
rationalities that simultaneously enable, reconfigure, and delimit its
meanings and modes of implementation.

**Keywords:** Internationalization at Home; Virtual Exchange;
Technological Higher Education

**Introduction**

Higher education internationalization has increasingly shifted beyond
physical mobility toward more inclusive and technology-mediated
approaches, such as IaH (Beelen & Jones, 2015) and Collaborative Online
International Learning (COIL) (Hackett et al., 2024; Leask, 2015; Rubin
& Guth, 2015; Succi Junior, 2020). In Brazilian public technological
higher education, these initiatives are particularly relevant, as many
students face financial, professional, and personal barriers to
participating in traditional mobility programs. Within the colleges'
network, International Collaborative Projects (PCIs), the institution's
local adaptation of COIL, have become an important strategy for
promoting intercultural engagement and global learning through virtual
collaboration.

While COIL has been widely adopted in higher education, less attention
has been paid to how such initiatives are discursively constructed and
legitimized within institutional contexts. Internationalization is not
only enacted through educational practices but also shaped by the ways
it is represented. In this regard, the Virtual Exchange Medium (VEm), a
bimonthly newsletter published in Portuguese and dedicated, since 2020,
to reporting PCI activities across the *Fatecs* system, provides a
valuable site for examining how virtual internationalization is framed
and institutionalized.

Using a Corpus Linguistics-informed approach, this study investigates
how PCIs/COIL are discursively recontextualized in the VEm corpus,
following these research questions:

1.  How are PCIs, based on the COIL approach, discursively represented
    in the VEm newsletter?

2.  What dominant discourses emerge in the institutional representation
    of COIL initiatives within the Fatecs system?

3.  How do these discourses recontextualize the meanings and purposes of
    IaH in the context of technological higher education?

By addressing these questions, the study contributes to discussions on
higher education IaH by showing how COIL is adapted, interpreted, and
legitimized within the specific context of Brazilian public
technological higher education.

**Theoretical Background**

## **IaH**

The concept of IaH (IaH) was first introduced by Nilsson (2003) and
later refined by Beelen and Jones (2015) as the purposeful integration
of international, intercultural, and global dimensions into the formal
and informal curriculum within domestic learning environments. What
stands out in this definition are the principles of intentionality and
inclusivity. Internationalization is not understood as incidental
exposure to international content but as a structured educational
strategy designed to ensure that all students, regardless of their
capacity to participate in physical mobility programs, can engage with
international and intercultural learning opportunities.

Within contemporary higher education, IaH has increasingly been
positioned as a response to the limitations of mobility-centered models
of internationalization. As scholars have argued (de Wit & Hunter, 2015;
Leask, 2015), international experiences abroad tend to benefit a
relatively small proportion of students and frequently reproduce
socioeconomic inequalities. In this context, IaH has emerged as a more
democratic and accessible approach to internationalization.

This concern is particularly relevant in the context of the colleges,
where a substantial proportion of students combine academic studies with
full-time employment and most students come from lower- and
middle-income households (Centro Paula Souza, 2024). For these students,
participation in traditional mobility programs is often financially or
logistically unfeasible. Consequently, institutional
internationalization strategies have increasingly focused on IaH, which
brings international and intercultural experiences into the curriculum
without requiring physical mobility. Virtual Exchange (VE) has emerged
as one of the primary means of operationalizing IaH, enabling
technology-mediated collaboration across borders within domestic
educational contexts.

Among the various approaches associated with IaH, VE has gained
particular prominence. Virtual Exchange refers to sustained,
technology-mediated interaction among participants from different
cultural and national backgrounds, organized around collaborative
learning objectives (O\'Dowd, 2018). Through structured online
collaboration, students can develop intercultural, linguistic, and
professional competencies without the need for physical mobility.

Within Centro Paula Souza, Virtual Exchange has been operationalized
through the International Collaborative Projects, which connect students
and faculty from Brazilian Fatecs with international partners through
collaborative academic activities conducted online. As a result, PCIs
have become one of the institution\'s primary initiatives for
implementing IaH at scale (Succi Junior, 2020).

As the number of PCIs has expanded, so too has the production of
institutional documents designed to communicate, regulate, promote, and
evaluate these initiatives. These texts constitute more than
administrative records. They are institutional discourses through which
virtual exchange is represented, legitimized, and operationalized.

**COIL and Virtual Exchange: Between Telecollaboration and Intercultural
Pedagogy**

COIL is situated within an interdisciplinary field that brings together
contributions from Applied Linguistics, intercultural pedagogy, and
higher education internationalization studies. Although it was formally
established as a methodology at the State University of New York (SUNY)
in 2006 under the leadership of Jon Rubin, its theoretical foundations
can be traced back to earlier traditions of virtual exchange and
technology-mediated collaborative learning.

One of the main roots of COIL can be found in telecollaboration, a field
that emerged in the 1990s through the work of Applied Linguistics
researchers interested in understanding how digital environments could
foster meaningful interactions among speakers of different languages and
cultures. Initiatives such as *e-tandem*, *teletandem* (Telles &
Vassallo, 2006), and other forms of online exchange were based on the
premise that technology-mediated interaction promotes not only language
learning but also the development of cultural and identity-related
competencies (Belz, 2003; O\'Dowd, 2007). From this perspective,
intercultural communication is no longer viewed merely as an exchange of
information; instead, it is conceived as a space for negotiating
meanings and co-constructing knowledge.

At the same time, the development of COIL is closely connected to the
tradition of intercultural pedagogy. Byram (1997) argues that
intercultural learning involves more than the acquisition of knowledge
about other cultures; it entails the development of intercultural
competence that fosters critical reflection on one's own culture and on
the relationships among different social groups. Kramsch\'s later work
shifts the focus from the metaphor of a *third place* to the development
of symbolic competence, understood as the ability to interpret,
negotiate, and critically engage with multiple symbolic systems and
discourses. This perspective conceives interculturality as a dynamic
process in which multilingual subjects construct fluid identities while
navigating diverse linguistic, cultural, and institutional contexts
(Kramsch, 2009, 2011).

It was within this theoretical context that Rubin (2017) developed the
COIL model, whose main innovation was to move international
collaboration beyond the specific domain of language education and into
the curriculum as a whole. What COIL proposes is that instructors from
different countries co-design academic activities integrated into
regular courses across a wide range of disciplines, including
engineering, health sciences, business, gastronomy, and technology. COIL
is primarily oriented toward the development of intercultural and
digital competencies, promoting collaborative online learning across
international educational contexts.

According to O\'Dowd (2018), Virtual Exchange includes initiatives such
as telecollaboration, teletandem, e-tandem, and COIL itself, all of
which share key characteristics, including technology-mediated
interaction, intercultural engagement, and pedagogical intentionality.

The growth of COIL has also been closely linked to the expansion of the
*IaH* agenda. According to Beelen and Jones (2015), internationalization
should not rely exclusively on physical mobility, given that only a
small proportion of students have access to international study-abroad
experiences. Consequently, the COIL model has become strongly associated
with principles of inclusion, democratization of access, and the
expansion of internationalization opportunities (De Wit & Hunter, 2015;
Leask, 2015).

More recently, the literature has emphasized COIL's contribution to the
development of global and professional competencies. Hackett et al.
(2024) identify four constitutive dimensions of the approach:
pedagogical intentionality, intercultural collaboration, technological
mediation, and curricular integration. From this perspective, COIL is
often described as a tool that fosters competencies valued in
contemporary professional contexts, such as teamwork in multicultural
settings, collaborative problem-solving, digital literacy, and
intercultural communication. This trend is particularly evident in
technological and vocational education institutions, where
internationalization is frequently aligned with employability goals and
preparation for globalized job markets.

However, the growth and institutionalization of COIL have also raised
critical concerns. Stein (2018) warns that interculturality may be
reduced to a professional competence, thereby emptying its critical and
transformative potential. Other studies highlight that the discourse of
democratization often obscures inequalities related to technological
access, linguistic proficiency, and institutional conditions of
participation (O\'Dowd, 2021). In addition, there is an increasing
alignment between internationalization, performance indicators, and
employability agendas, bringing COIL closer to discourses characteristic
of contemporary higher education management.

This study addresses a persistent and under-theorized gap in the COIL
and virtual internationalization literature. Despite the rapid expansion
of research on COIL over the past decade, the field remains
predominantly oriented toward evaluative and outcome-based perspectives,
particularly those concerned with pedagogical effectiveness, student
engagement, and the development of intercultural competencies. As a
result, COIL is still largely conceptualized as a pedagogical innovation
instead of a discursively constructed and institutionally mediated
phenomenon.

This dominant orientation has left relatively unexplored the ways in
which COIL is translated, reframed, and recontextualized as it moves
across institutional settings, policy environments, and local
educational regimes. Instead of being simply adopted, COIL is
discursively reconstructed, acquiring new meanings, functions, and
organizational purposes that reflect the rationalities of the
institutions in which it is implemented. In fact, there are not enough
studies that examine the discursive conditions under which COIL is made
intelligible, operationalized, and legitimized within specific
institutional contexts, especially in non-elite and vocationally
oriented higher education systems in the Global South.

By shifting analytical attention to this issue, the present study
changes the focus from COIL as an educational intervention to COIL as a
discursive and institutional object, examining how it is constructed and
negotiated within this Brazilian public technological higher education.

### **Internationalization of Public Technological Higher Education in Fatecs**

The Colleges of Technology in the state of São Paulo, Brazil, form a
public higher education network serving a predominantly working student
population, most of whom come from low-income households, with family
incomes generally below five minimum wages (Centro Paula Souza, 2024).
These socioeconomic conditions significantly limit opportunities for
physical mobility, making international experiences inaccessible to many
students. Within this setting, IaH provides an alternative pathway for
expanding access to international and intercultural learning.

At the same time, the colleges present a distinctive feature within
Brazilian higher education: the compulsory inclusion of English and
Spanish language courses in the curricula of some programs. This
institutional arrangement creates structural affordances for the
development of internationalization initiatives. Among these
initiatives, PCIs stand out as practices of virtual exchange mediated by
international networks and supported by institutional policies for
faculty development, pedagogical implementation, and monitoring.

The consolidation of PCIs has not followed a linear trajectory; it has
resulted from a gradual process of institutionalization that ranges from
isolated initiatives led by pioneering faculty members to their
incorporation into formal governance structures within the institution.
This movement entails a shift from localized pedagogical practices to an
institutional architecture of internationalization, mediated by
administrative coordination, international collaboration networks, and
formal monitoring instruments.

Within this context, the VEm newsletter constitutes a privileged site
for the circulation and stabilization of institutional discourse on
internationalization. It does not function merely as an informational
channel, but operates as an institutional device that constructs
narratives of internationalization, innovation, and pedagogical
transformation.

This framework gives rise to a significant analytical tension: to what
extent does the discourse of internationalization circulated in the VEm
newsletter both reflect and reconfigure the material and institutional
conditions of PCI implementation, while amplifying or performing
institutional narratives of internationalization? This study therefore
seeks to investigate the relationship between material conditions,
institutional practices, and the discursive construction of
internationalization within the context of these colleges.

**VEm -- Virtual Exchange Medium and the Recontextualization of Virtual
Exchange Practices**

The VEm newsletter is a bimonthly institutional publication produced by
the technical unit for Support for the Internationalization of Higher
Education (AIES), within the Division of Extension and Research in
Higher Education (Depes) and the General Coordination of Undergraduate
Higher Education (CGESG) at the institution. Within this institutional
architecture, International Collaborative Projects (PCIs) are formally
situated within AIES/Depes/CGESG, reflecting their embeddedness in a
highly stratified administrative structure.

PCIs constitute the institution\'s local implementation of IaH, an
approach that seeks to provide international and intercultural learning
opportunities without requiring physical mobility. One of the principal
strategies for achieving this objective is VE, which promotes
technology-mediated interaction among participants from different
countries. \"Collaborative Online International Learning (COIL)
represents a structured model of VE in which international partner
instructors co-design and implement collaborative projects grounded in,
but not limited to, their respective course disciplines.\"At the
institution, these principles have been adapted into the PCI model, a
localized form of COIL and VE designed to promote IaH across the Fatecs
network.

Although PCIs are internationally recognized as COIL or Virtual Exchange
initiatives, their implementation within the colleges involves a process
of contextual adaptation often referred to as "tropicalization" (Succi
2020). This process raises important analytical questions regarding how
globally circulating models of internationalization are recontextualized
within local institutional settings and how such recontextualization may
transform both the meaning and the function of these pedagogical
approaches.

Framed within discourses of global citizenship education and IaH, PCIs
are presented as enabling students to engage in collaborative academic
activities aligned with curricular content while fostering digital,
linguistic, intercultural, and teamwork competencies. However, this
framing also invites critical examination of how such competencies are
discursively constructed as evidence of institutional
internationalization and how these narratives circulate through official
communication channels such as the VEm newsletter.

This configuration gives rise to an important analytical question: how
does the institutional discourse surrounding PCIs negotiate the tension
between globally circulating models of virtual exchange and their
localized reconfiguration within a highly structured administrative
framework, thereby shaping the discursive construction of
internationalization in the VEm newsletter?

**Corpus Linguistics**

Corpus Linguistics is an area within Applied Linguistics concerned with
the analysis of corpora, understood as systematically compiled
collections of spoken and written texts used for linguistic research
(Berber Sardinha, 2004). While corpora are often defined as representing
"authentic" language use produced by real speakers, such notion requires
critical scrutiny, particularly in institutional contexts where texts
are highly mediated, edited, and shaped by organizational purposes. In
this study, VEm newsletters are treated as a corpus, not as a
transparent reflection of linguistic reality, but as a structured site
of institutional discourse production.

The development of Corpus Linguistics has been closely associated with
the empirical turn in linguistics and the rejection of purely
introspective methods. However, the assumption that computational tools
merely enable more efficient analysis overlooks the fact that they also
participate in shaping what counts as analyzable linguistic evidence.
Word lists, concordancers, and other tools do not simply reveal
patterns; they also construct them through specific operationalizations
of language data.

Corpus Linguistics is often described as adopting an empiricist and
probabilistic view of language, in which linguistic patterns are
considered systematic and statistically observable. However, such a
perspective raises methodological questions regarding the relationship
between frequency and meaning, as well as the extent to which
distributional patterns can be directly interpreted as social or
ideological regularities.

The integration of Corpus Linguistics with Critical Discourse Analysis
(CDA) (Baker et al., 2008) has been proposed as a way of addressing the
limitations of each approach. While CDA provides tools for interpreting
discourse within its social and ideological contexts, it has often been
criticized for limited datasets and selective textual evidence.
Conversely, corpus-based approaches enable the analysis of larger
datasets, but risk reducing discourse to quantifiable patterns if not
theoretically contextualized. What emerges is not a simple integration
but a productive epistemological negotiation between approaches,
especially in relation to scale, representation, and interpretation.

In this study, we adopt a corpus-assisted CDA approach to analyze 33
editions of the VEm newsletter (70,933 words). However, this corpus is
not understood as a statistically representative sample of language use
in general, but as a bounded institutional archive through which
discourses of internationalization are produced, circulated, and
stabilized over time. Consequently, methodological decisions regarding
corpus design, inclusion criteria, and analytical procedures are not
neutral, but directly implicated in the construction of the object of
study (Biber et al., 1998).

**Methodology**

The VEm corpus in this study was compiled by collecting all VEm
newsletters published from June 2020 to February 2026 (issues 1-33) and
downloaded from the website
[[https://publicacoescgesg.cps.sp.gov.br/VEm/issue/archive]{.underline}](https://publicacoescgesg.cps.sp.gov.br/VEm/issue/archive).
Each edition was saved as a PDF file and named numerically according to
its issue number. For example, issue 1 was named VEm_01.

We concluded that 33 files would account for a relatively small number
for a discourse analysis based on Corpus Linguistics (McEnery & Hardie,
2012). Therefore, we decided to segment each edition into separate files
according to the different texts. As a result, each text became a single
file, totaling 180 files. The corpus can be seen in Table 1.

The textual data analyzed in this study consisted of PDF documents whose
embedded text layers did not reliably preserve the original reading
order, as the files had been created using page-layout software. Textual
elements were often fragmented or interleaved, making direct extraction
unsuitable for corpus analysis. To address this issue, a multi-stage
reconstruction and verification pipeline was developed.

First, each PDF was divided into individual pages, which were processed
as separate units. For every page, two representations were generated: a
conventional text extraction from the PDF and a high-resolution image
for image-based optical character recognition (OCR). In the second
stage, the page images were processed using ChatGPT's 5.2 Pro
vision-based OCR capabilities. A structured prompt guided the
reconstruction of the page content, and the output was returned in JSON
format containing titles, headings, and paragraph-level text. To ensure
that the corpus contained only communicative content, recurring layout
elements such as mastheads, footers, captions, and other non-prose
artifacts were excluded from the extraction process.

Given the well-known possibility that large language models may
introduce lexical items not present in the original source, an
additional verification stage was implemented. The reconstructed OCR
text was compared against the reference text extracted directly from the
PDF. Token-level comparisons were performed between the two versions of
each page. Tokens present in the OCR reconstruction but absent in the
PDF text were flagged as potential hallucinations or recognition errors.
For example, in one instance the OCR system produced aumentado
('increased') where the original token was aumentando ('increasing').
Such discrepancies typically arise from ambiguity in character
recognition (e.g., confusion between visually similar character
sequences such as -s and -cs or -do and -ndo). To assist with
identifying these discrepancies, normalized Levenshtein distance was
used to measure similarity between tokens in the OCR output and those in
the reference extraction. In addition, keyword-in-context (KWIC)
displays were generated so that each flagged token could be examined
within its surrounding textual environment. These contextual displays
allowed the researcher to determine whether a token represented a
plausible OCR recognition error, a minor orthographic deviation, or a
lexical insertion introduced during the reconstruction process. Manual
corrections were then recorded and applied to the reconstructed text.

After manual review and correction, the cleaned page-level texts were
recombined into complete documents corresponding to the original PDFs.
The final corpus was stored in tabular format, enabling subsequent
analyses such as frequency, keyword, and collocation studies.

Keyword analysis was used to identify lexically salient items. Instead
of comparing the corpus to an external reference corpus, each newsletter
issue was treated in turn as a study corpus against a reference corpus
composed of the remaining 32 issues. This approach captured patterns of
lexical salience within the institutional discourse itself. The
procedure generated 33 keyword lists, which were subsequently merged
into a single list.

To strengthen analytical reliability, a dispersion filter was applied:
only keywords occurring in at least five documents (approximately 3% of
the corpus) were retained. This threshold prioritized recurrent lexical
patterns while reducing the influence of highly localized occurrences.
At the same time, it is recognized that such filtering shapes the
analytical object by privileging recurring institutional discourse and
excluding low-frequency items that may still hold contextual or
ideological significance. After filtering, 23 keywords remained and were
used as the analytical variables for the subsequent discourse analysis
(see Table 2).

Collocational patterns were examined using the log-dice association
measure, implemented in AntConc (Anthony, 2020). The log-dice score was
used to quantify the strength of association between lexical items
within a defined window span, allowing for the identification of
statistically salient co-occurrence patterns.

It is important to distinguish between keyword status and collocational
strength: while keyword analysis captures distributional salience across
documents, collocation analysis captures relational proximity between
lexical items within textual environments. These two levels of analysis
are treated as complementary but analytically distinct.

To contextualize lexical and collocational findings, concordance
analysis was conducted using AntConc's Concordance and File View tools.
Keyword occurrences were examined in their immediate co-text, enabling
qualitative interpretation of usage patterns.

Following extraction and filtering, the keywords were grouped into three
discursive clusters based on their functions and contexts of use across
the corpus. This categorization was informed by concordance analysis and
refined through iterative interpretation.

The analysis combines quantitative corpus methods with qualitative
discourse interpretation within a corpus-assisted discourse studies
(CADS) framework. Instead of treating these components as separate
stages, the approach views them as mutually constitutive in the
production of discourse-analytical findings.

Adopting a corpus-assisted CDA perspective, the study links large-scale
pattern identification with context-sensitive interpretation. Corpus
linguistics provides systematic evidence of distributional patterns,
while CDA enables their critical examination in relation to
institutional and ideological contexts. This integration also
acknowledges methodological tensions between frequency and meaning, and
between distributional regularities and discursive interpretation.

Accordingly, the VEm corpus is not treated as a transparent
representation of institutional discourse, but as a structured and
mediated site of discourse production in which institutional meanings
are constructed, circulated, and stabilized.

The following section presents the results of the corpus analysis,
focusing on the identified keywords, their dispersion patterns, and
their discursive organization across the VEm newsletter corpus.

**Discussion and Results**

The analysis suggests that the VEm newsletter does not merely report
PCI/COIL initiatives, but actively participates in the construction of
institutional meanings surrounding internationalization. Although COIL
is internationally associated with principles such as intercultural
dialogue, reciprocity, and collaborative knowledge construction, its
representation within the newsletter reflects a process of discursive
recontextualization through which global educational models are adapted
to local institutional priorities.

This raises an important analytical question: which discourses of COIL
are foregrounded, transformed, or marginalized in this process of
institutional translation? Instead of appearing as neutral descriptions
of practice, the recurring discursive configurations identified in the
corpus may be understood as manifestations of broader institutional
rationalities that shape how internationalization is imagined,
justified, and operationalized within public technological higher
education.

The three discursive configurations identified in the corpus
(Institutional Management of COIL Projects, IaH, and Technological
Education and Vocational Training) should therefore not be viewed merely
as thematic categories. Instead, they may be interpreted as competing
yet interconnected frameworks through which the meaning and purpose of
PCI/COIL are negotiated. Their coexistence suggests that
internationalization is simultaneously constructed as an administrative
project, a strategy for widening access to global experiences, and a
mechanism for workforce preparation.

In this sense, the findings invite reflection not only on how
internationalization is represented in institutional discourse, but also
on the institutional priorities and assumptions that such
representations help legitimize and reproduce.

**Discourse 1 - Institutional Management of COIL Projects**

The first discursive configuration identified in the VEm corpus is
labeled *Institutional Management of COIL Projects*. This label is not
treated as an inherent property of the data, but as an interpretive
construct derived from recurring distributional patterns identified
through keyword analysis, dispersion mapping, and concordance
examination.

Across the corpus, a set of lexical items related to planning,
temporality, evaluation, and institutional coordination systematically
co-occurs in contexts in which Collaborative International Projects
(PCIs) and COIL initiatives are described. These include terms such as
*goal*, *operation*, *panel*, *annual*, *performance*, *dedication*,
*school term*, *October*, and *September*. Their distribution suggests a
recurrent alignment between internationalization initiatives and
institutional cycles of organization, monitoring, and temporal
structuring.

This pattern is particularly visible in procedural descriptions of
project activities, in which COIL initiatives are organized through
sequential steps, comparative tasks, and structured phases of
implementation. In one instance, student collaboration is framed through
operational procedures involving the selection of technical objects,
comparative analysis of products across countries, and systematic data
collection on functionality and maintenance. Excerpt 1 situates the
project within clearly defined temporal phases and iterative redesign
across editions of the initiative. The following excerpts were
translated, and the words in bold represent this discourse:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Excerpt 1 --** In 2019, the student groups (10 Americans and 17
  Brazilians) discussed the equipment they were familiar with and
  compared brands and prices in both countries. Each team selected a
  piece of equipment and gathered information about its functioning,
  **operation**, and maintenance. The second edition of the project, held
  during the second semester of 2020, will address the context of
  COVID-19: students will discuss the situations experienced in the two
  countries and, in particular, the protocols for sanitizing medical
  equipment. (VEm -- Issue 02 - News section)
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Instead of portraying international collaboration solely as a
pedagogical activity, these passages frame it through procedural and
managerial logics that emphasize planning, coordination, and
institutional control.

A second recurring pattern is the association of COIL/PCI initiatives
with strategic planning, goal-setting, and adaptive management. Across
the corpus, successful implementation is frequently linked to clearly
defined objectives, structured processes, and the capacity to adjust
projects when necessary, as illustrated in the Excerpt 2:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

measurable process aligned with institutional goals and organizational

Taken together, these patterns suggest that COIL/PCI initiatives are
articulated through a discourse of institutional organization, in which
planning, evaluation, recognition, and temporality play important roles.
However, this discourse is not homogeneous. Managerial, pedagogical, and
internationalization logics coexist in different configurations across
the corpus, depending on the genre, issue, and communicative purpose.

From this perspective, the label *Institutional Management of COIL
Projects* is understood as a corpus-derived analytical construct
emerging from recurring lexical, temporal, and evaluative patterns
instead of an externally imposed theoretical category. Its prominence is
further reinforced by the editorial architecture of the newsletter
itself, which includes intentionally designed sections that articulate
institutional perspectives (e.g., Editor's Letter) and highlight "Best
Practices" as models for the implementation of future COIL initiatives.

At the same time, the analysis reveals a constitutive tension in the
representation of PCIs/COIL initiatives. While grounded in intercultural
collaboration and pedagogical exchange, these initiatives are also
embedded in institutional frameworks that emphasize planning,
measurability, scalability, and accountability. This tension does not
eliminate the pedagogical dimension of COIL but shapes how
internationalization becomes visible, manageable, and communicable
within the VEm newsletter.

**Discourse 2 - IaH**

The second axis, IaH, encompasses discourses that highlight the
collaborative, inclusive, and relational dimensions of PCIs/COIL.
Internationalization is portrayed as an accessible experience, enabled
by digital technologies and supported by global networks of cooperation.
Diversity, expanded access, and the engagement of faculty and students
emerge as central elements, aligning the institutional discourse with
the core principles of IaH.

**Keywords:** inclusion, free, audience, reception, office, poster,
hour, every, less

An open and connected model of international education grounded in
collaboration, expanded access, and the global circulation of knowledge.

this framing, COIL/PCI is represented as an expanded mechanism for
international experience beyond traditional mobility programs.

However, this discourse also abstracts from unequal conditions of
access. By emphasizing openness and intercultural exchange, it tends to
portray participation as universally available while downplaying
technological, linguistic, institutional, and geopolitical asymmetries.
As a result, it constructs an idealized vision of global educational
participation.

Overall, the IaH discourse plays a dual role in the VEm corpus: it
legitimizes COIL through values such as inclusion and diversity, while
also promoting a universalized narrative that partially obscures
structural inequalities, simultaneously democratizing and depoliticizing
internationalization.

**Discourse 3 - Technological Education and Vocational Training**

The third axis, Technological Education and Vocational Training,
recontextualizes COIL/PCI as an instrument for internationally oriented
technical qualification. Intercultural collaboration is primarily
mobilized to develop work-related competencies, including technical,
digital, collaborative, and intercultural skills. PCIs are thus
integrated into the colleges' educational mission as a strategic
resource for training technologists, linking pedagogical innovation with
employability.

**Keywords:** technologist, equipment, energy, medical, logistics

  from one Brazilian campus: participants included the professors of
  International Marketing, International Business and English. According
  to the course coordinator, the initiative helps future
  **technologists** develop "intercultural and interpersonal

The emphasis on teamwork, problem-solving, technology use, and
intercultural competencies frames internationalization less as cultural
engagement and more as a tool for developing skills valued in
contemporary job markets. Interculturality is thus positioned less as an
educational end in itself and more as a professional competency linked
to future employability.

The valorization of practical and technological education focuses on
applicability, technical innovation, and practice-oriented academic
production, with education understood as training for technical

The analysis shows that the VEm newsletter constructs PCIs/COIL as a
practice-oriented professional training tool aligned with technological
education. The reported experiences emphasize competencies such as
teamwork, problem-solving, and technology use, framing
internationalization not only as an intercultural experience but also as
a strategic resource for technical qualification.

This discourse shifts the emphasis of COIL from intercultural engagement
as an end in itself toward its instrumental value in developing
professional competencies, thereby aligning internationalization with
the vocational mission of the colleges. The emphasis on technological
programs, interdisciplinary articulation (e.g., marketing, business, and
English), and evaluation tools highlights concerns with effectiveness
and continuous improvement, aligning COIL with evidence-based
pedagogical innovation.

PCIs/COIL is also framed as part of an ecosystem of applied academic
production, where theory and practice are integrated in real or
simulated professional contexts. Education is thus conceived as direct
preparation for technical work, closely aligned with contemporary
demands for employability and global competence.

The recurrent references to project management, technological learning,
academic outputs, and measurable outcomes indicate a growing alignment
between internationalization and performance-oriented educational
practices. COIL success is often assessed through tangible results,
skills acquisition, and institutional performance, reflecting a
managerial view of educational innovation.

Overall, internationalization is increasingly subordinated to workforce
preparation. Instead of being a space for critical engagement with
cultural difference or global inequalities, PCIs/COIL is mainly framed
as an initiative for producing job-relevant competencies, which reflects
broader neoliberal shifts in higher education, where
internationalization is legitimized through employability, innovation,
and competitiveness. Interculturality is thus redefined as a
transferable skill, with cultural encounters functioning as resources
for human capital development, which narrows its critical and
emancipatory potential.

Taken together, the three axes reveal a hybrid discursive field in VEm,
where intercultural pedagogical ideals coexist and often tension with
managerial logics and applied training goals. These rationalities
overlap and sometimes conflict in the construction of IaH at the
colleges.

**Conclusions**

The discursive analysis of the VEm newsletter reveals that International
Collaborative Projects (PCIs), grounded in the COIL approach, are
constructed through three interconnected discourses: Institutional
Management of COIL Projects, IaH, and Technological Education and
Vocational Training. Together, these discourses shape how virtual
internationalization is understood and legitimized within the Fatecs
system.

The first discourse frames COIL as a governable educational intervention
organized through planning, monitoring, evaluation, and institutional
coordination. This managerial logic supports the institutionalization,
visibility, and scalability of IaH initiatives while aligning
internationalization with broader principles of organizational
management and accountability.

The second discourse reflects the normative foundations of COIL and IaH
by representing internationalization as an inclusive, collaborative, and
digitally mediated experience. Diversity, inclusion, and intercultural
exchange are important values, positioning virtual internationalization
as a democratizing force. However, this representation tends to obscure
inequalities related to technological access, linguistic capital, and
institutional resources, presenting participation as more universally
accessible than it may be in practice.

The third discourse recontextualizes COIL as a mechanism for developing
competencies valued in global labor markets. Intercultural collaboration
is primarily legitimized through its contribution to technical, digital,
collaborative, and intercultural skills, reinforcing the vocational
orientation of technological higher education. As a result,
interculturality is increasingly represented as professional capital
instead of transformative educational experience.

Taken together, these discourses reveal a hybrid formation in which
pedagogical, managerial, and vocational rationalities coexist. Yet
managerial and employability-oriented logics increasingly function as
the primary frameworks through which internationalization is
legitimized. Intercultural collaboration acquires institutional value
largely through its contribution to measurable outcomes, organizational
efficiency, and professional qualification.

The findings suggest that IaH is not a neutral or universally enacted
process but a context-dependent practice shaped by institutional
priorities and competing organizational rationalities. Instead of being
transferred unchanged across settings, COIL is continuously reconfigured
according to local governance structures, educational missions, and
professional training goals.

More broadly, the study highlights how contemporary internationalization
is increasingly expected to be inclusive, measurable, scalable, and
economically relevant simultaneously. While these objectives may
coexist, they also generate tensions. The analysis therefore raises
important questions about what is transformed, or potentially lost, when
internationalization becomes closely linked to governance, performance,
and employability. Future research should move beyond assessing whether
virtual exchange promotes internationalization and examine more
critically which forms of internationalization are being produced and
legitimized through these practices.

**Recommendations**

Based on the findings, institutions implementing COIL and related
virtual exchange initiatives should monitor how internationalization is
represented in institutional communication alongside how projects are
organized and evaluated. Institutional documentation can make explicit
the intercultural and pedagogical purposes of virtual exchange while
also acknowledging differences in technological access, linguistic
resources, and participation conditions. Future institutional planning
may benefit from combining indicators of project implementation and
professional learning with evidence of intercultural learning and
student experience. For research, longitudinal corpus-based analyses of
institutional communication can help examine how the meanings and
functions of virtual internationalization change as programs become more
established.
